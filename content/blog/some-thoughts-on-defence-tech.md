> **Note:** I started writing this essay a month or two ago, and since then I’ve accepted a software job at Helsing, one of Europe’s most prominent defence neo-primes. I thought that this would be a good time to dust it off and finish it. You might be suspicious that the conclusion for this essay was predetermined given I’m locked into working there next year. I’m aware of this bias. The best I can say is that I had already thought a lot about defence before applying to Helsing, and I applied there because I had concluded that working in defence was important. The bones of this essay were already done a while back. I’m excited to see what working in defence is really like.

I’ve been building autonomous drones recently, as part of my work in Imperial’s Aerial Robotics research lab. We’ve made decent progress on the project. In fact we just got obstacle avoidance working a few days ago after many, many crashed drones and broken propellers.

The end goal for the research is to deploy a drone in the rainforest, where it could navigate through the canopy and through foliage to find the ideal spot to land, shut off and monitor the environment around it.

I think it’s a very interesting project. It has many unsolved frontier problems that we must tackle to get it working in the field. Solving these problems would be immensely consequential for the field of physical AI; it gets us closer to a world where robots and drones can automate more and more economically useful tasks.

One of the key problems we have to solve is reliable GPS-denied autonomy in sparse lighting conditions. And whenever I talk to people about this project, as soon as I utter the words “GPS-denied”, the first thing they say is something along the lines of, “Can’t this also be used in the military?”

The answer is obviously yes. In fact it’s probably the single biggest use case for GPS-denied tech.

It’s become clear to me that my work has military value. It’s prompted me to give a great deal of thought to the ethics behind defence tech.

Let’s start with some basic premises of my moral framework:

1. Starting a war is almost always unethical
2. The fewer people that are killed, the better.
3. Freedom and democracy are foundational for human wellbeing
4. We should work towards a world where peace, co-operation and prosperity for all are the norm.

Let me explain them one by one.

## Starting a war is almost always unethical.

There’s almost no reason for starting a war or inflicting violence on other humans. I’ll get to why I say “almost” in a second, but throughout history and modern times, humans have primarily gone to war for 3 reasons: For economic gain. For power, pride and hyper-nationalist ideologies. Or because of fear and distrust in other powers.

Economic gain isn’t a valid reason anymore. Historically if you didn’t have much fertile land or didn’t control important trade routes, your options for getting the resources you needed were scarce. You’d have to launch wars just to be able to get enough resources to put food on your plate. Thankfully global trade and capitalism have greatly diminished the need to do this. You can import the resources you need and provide services to the global economy even if you are geographically unlucky. In fact it’s actually made launching wars for economic gain highly counterproductive thanks to economic interdependence. It changes the calculus for leaders who might’ve been considering a war for economic gain.

Power, pride and hyper-nationalist ideologies are also not ethically permissible. Many hyper-nationalist and extremist religious ideologies often portray another group as “inferior” or “uncivilised”, which often justifies war in a dictator’s eyes. Or they use these excuses to fulfil their own imperialist desires. It doesn't need to be said that these ideologies are always false, biologically and philosophically.

The last is fear and mutual distrust. This is harder to oppose as a reason for starting a war. Pre-emptive strikes can reasonably be seen as defensive measures if you believe you will be attacked first. If you believe that there’s an imminent invasion or attack, attacking first to stop the destruction of your own people could be seen as ethically permissible. My only counterargument is that diplomacy should come before you resort to missiles. Defence through the military has to be the last resort. Then again, is there really much diplomacy you can do if a dictator is hell bent on conquest? That I’m not sure of.

Now why do I say “almost”? I’m not justifying rolling tanks over borders, I’m more so talking about foreign interventions. Is it fair to stop foreign terrorists from attacking innocent civilians? If the terrorists aren’t directly attacking you, what gives you the moral right to attack them? Then again if choosing to do nothing results in countless civilian deaths, don’t you have a moral obligation to step in? I’m not sure where I stand here: on one hand interventions can save lives, on the other hand it can backfire and cause those who weren’t previously hostile to you to become hostile. I’ll keep thinking about this, but for now I’ll leave these questions to brighter minds than mine.

## The fewer people that are killed, the better.

Not much explanation needed here.

## Freedom and democracy are foundational for human wellbeing

This one is slightly more up for debate, only just. How do you define freedom? How do you define democracy? Is freedom the ability to be protected from arbitrary state detention? Or is it the freedom to walk through your city at night without fear? Is democracy having your government actually work for the people or merely the ability to vote leaders out?

Extensive evidence shows that the Western definitions of freedom and democracy result in nations that are healthier, wealthier and more prosperous. China is perhaps challenging this, although they are yet to prove they can fully match Western standards. But the gap is closing fast. They have pulled off remarkable feats like lifting 800 million people out of extreme poverty and achieving exceptionally low levels of violent crime. Many Western democracies can’t claim the same.

China’s model obviously has massive downsides. One only has to look at their abysmal human rights record to see that.

But the biggest downside to dictatorships is instability. There are few checks and balances that prevent a dictator from abusing a population. When the entire stability and wellbeing of a population depends on one man, there is quite literally a single point of failure. The CCP claims that its system is more stable than “chaotic Western democracies”. However its population of 1.4 billion people is entirely at the mercy of Xi Jinping’s goodwill. That, to me, is far more unstable than a democracy that has release valves, and checks and balances that limit a tyrant’s ability to damage a population.

## We should work towards a world where peace, co-operation and prosperity for all are the norm.

Again not much explanation needed here. A world where people solve disagreements with words rather than missiles is preferable.

So does building and selling weapons to NATO countries violate any of these principles?

## Do NATO countries ever start wars?

Yes and no. I believe NATO countries rarely start wars against an actor that isn’t a direct or indirect threat to democratic countries. I say “rarely” because there are some obvious counterexamples: the most prominent one being the US-led Iraq war.

In retrospect that invasion was one of the least justified in modern times but democratic countries aren’t infallible; at least we can hold the leaders who execute poor decisions accountable. Sure, the people who instigated the Iraq war weren’t held to much accountability, however I consider that a failure of the public. The lack of accountability wasn’t because people didn’t have the freedoms to criticise their leaders, it’s because not enough people used those freedoms to do so. The counterpoint to this is that many, many lives were lost in unjustified military operations, like the Iraq war. While democracies can prevent future mistakes by going to the polls, the people who bear the brunt of those mistakes pay with their lives. They aren’t the ones who can go to the polls, yet they pay the price. Should we enable governments to have that sort of life changing power over others by arming them? It’s an open question, without a clean answer.

But, if the bar for selling weapons to democracies is perfect morals, then what happens to justified military operations? How do we execute an operation we desperately have to with no weapons? Do we just let our allies and fellow humans fall to bad actors because we refuse to arm free countries that could potentially make a bad decision? That would probably result in a whole lot more suffering globally.

In healthy democracies, the people have the power. If you disagree with current foreign policy, then go ahead to the polls. If you want a different tax system, go ahead to the polls. The point is that democracies are self correcting. Even if they aren’t perfect today, the choice is up to the people if they are better tomorrow. Autocracies don’t have that choice; their foreign policy is usually up to one man. And dictators are often self selecting for traits like Machiavellianism, extreme hubris and a lack of empathy. Not a great combination for peace and stability.

So yes, NATO countries have started wars that were probably unjustified. But they are self correcting, we can fix our mistakes, and the result of not arming democracies today is perhaps far worse.

## Does arming democracies result in fewer deaths globally?

People who disagree point to wars and interventions that NATO countries have executed that have resulted in widespread suffering. There is no doubt that this is true. But let’s look at the converse scenario. Look at what Russia did in Ukraine. Clearly for dictators, rolling tanks over borders like it’s 1939 is not a thing of the past. It’s widely believed that Putin has imperialistic ambitions and wants to expand his sphere of influence over all former Soviet states. Now let’s assume that the firepower of NATO is a fraction of what it is today. It’s safe to say that Putin would probably not have many moral qualms about invading all former Soviet states, especially knowing that he would face almost no resistance from any other military equal. He would continue his campaign of violence, torture and subjugation of many Eastern European populations. The only reason he doesn’t is because he’s somewhat rational, and has calculated he would almost certainly lose a direct confrontation with NATO, due to its extreme military superiority.

You can see the same deterrence effect in the Asia-Pacific region too. One of the biggest reasons China doesn’t move in on Taiwan is because of the credible threat of US intervention. China knows that it would be devastated by a direct confrontation with the USA. Same thing with North and South Korea. And anywhere else that falls under the West’s security umbrella.

So, to me, a world where you refuse to arm free countries is by far a more violent and bloody world than one where you do.

## Does arming a democracy negatively affect the wellbeing of its population?

Not really. How well a democracy treats its people is fundamentally up to the people. As long as you can peacefully change the government by going to the polls, the weapons have no effect on the day to day of the population. If the government were to use weapons to subjugate the population, you would no longer have a free country, and selling weapons to that government would no longer be justified.

## Does arming democracies work towards a world where peace, co-operation and prosperity for all are the norm?

This is a tricky one. There isn’t really a known answer here. Typically when competing nations see their competitor re-arm, they see it as a threat, which forces them to re-arm too. This often accelerates distrust and can increase the risk of miscalculation which can quickly spiral into a broader conflict. This is known as the security dilemma, and it’s a real concern. However, as I mentioned before, a world where we refuse to arm free countries is highly unlikely to be a more peaceful world. And broadly speaking within the borders of democracies, populations are much more prosperous than those in authoritarian counterparts. There’s also strong evidence for democratic peace theory, the idea that co-operation between democracies is far more likely than democracies waging war with each other. The logical extension of this is that if all the world's countries were free and democratic, there would be barely any war. There isn’t a known answer to this question, but I think given today’s geopolitical reality, it’s unfeasible and undesirable to wind down NATO’s military anyway.

## A note on autonomous weapons

Few serious thinkers would argue for shutting the military down completely. But one area that (rightfully) gets more attention is the use of autonomy in war. It’s the most relevant to what I’m building and what I’d be working on next year, so I will mention it briefly.

Let’s stick to the premise that deterrence is the main reason building weapons is justified. Deterrence theory only works as long as we possess the ability to make an attack costly for an aggressor. I doubt autocracies will be paralysed by the ethical implications of autonomous weapons. If they freely develop this tech and it proves to vastly outmatch our conventional weapons, deterrence fails to hold. I think this alone justifies the development of this tech. Yes this is another version of the security dilemma, but I think deterrence here wins out because developing autonomous weapons can provide dictatorships with orders of magnitude of military superiority over those that stick with conventional weapons. That doesn’t give us a blank cheque to freely develop autonomous weapons, we should still consider how to build them ethically.

For example I think AI making the final decision to kill should be firmly off the table. In wartime scenarios the cost of a wrong decision can be catastrophic. Human judgement, while not infallible, is at least accountable and can consider the emotional and ethical questions of taking another life, something that is hard to delegate to indifferent, calculating algorithms.

The ethics of autonomous weapons in war is very much open and we’re getting new evidence of the implications of autonomous weapons from modern conflicts. I’m very open to changing my views and thinking about this more. The above is just my gut instinct and thoughts towards this topic.

## Final words

I’ve thought about this topic a lot. I'm open to having my views changed again but right now I have strong conviction that defence work is incredibly important for the world right now. I think democracy is worth defending. This also means that democracy should be defended from the inside too. Many of the above points rely on being able to hold leaders accountable and democracy being self correcting. This relies on having a healthy democracy in the first place. We must protect our democracies from democratic backsliding by staying informed of political issues and exercising our freedoms to vote and criticise those in power where they misstep.

A couple of years ago I would’ve laughed at the idea that I’d one day work in defence. Now I’m very excited about the prospect of working at Helsing next year.
